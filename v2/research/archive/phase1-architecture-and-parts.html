Finding 1 — the rear motor problem is real

Your instinct was right, and it's worse than a packaging annoyance. On a rear geared hub you need three things to coexist on one hub: the cassette/freehub (drive side, right), the disc caliper (non-drive side, left), and a brake surface driven by the planetary carrier that a caliper can actually reach.

Which side the gear cover sits on turns out to be model-dependent. Sources conflict: one teardown guide has you working from the freewheel side with the cassette removed; VoltBike's service doc for their rear hub explicitly says the freewheel does not need to come off. So it varies — and it has to be verified per motor, by opening one.

Correction — read Grin's page, not the summary

An earlier draft of this document claimed Grin had shipped a front Freegen motor and declined to offer rear. Both halves were wrong. Checking their product page directly: the motors are in active development, not shipped — pre-order deposits only, first units targeted "before the end of 2026," and no price set ("it's too early to say now"). And Grin does offer rear Freegen braking.

Their rear product is the Magic Drive hub — 135 mm axle, 4.2 kg, 7.8:1 stepped planet gears. Freegen braking is a listed option on it, and the way they resolve the side conflict is the part I'd assumed was unavailable: a right-side (drive-side) mounted brake caliper. The kit ships with a right-side caliper mount specifically for this. They also list an automatic ABS mode for Freegen braking, and the internal clutch becomes optional — present for the Freegen brake option, absent for the coaster/reverse option.

So rear is not blocked. It costs you a non-standard drive-side caliper that needs frame clearance and a custom mount, on a heavier motor — but it is a solved geometry, and I shouldn't have told you otherwise.

The internal-band variant is still worth noting as a third rear layout: a band or drum on the carrier actuated through the hollow axle, no caliper and no side conflict at all. Closer to your existing band design than a disc conversion would be. But heat has nowhere to go inside a sealed hub and you can't inspect the friction surface without a teardown — a later experiment, not a v2 foundation.

Finding 2 — front is the correct answer on braking physics

The usual case for a rear motor is acceleration traction. But this system's primary job is braking, and braking inverts the argument. Under deceleration, load transfers forward; the front tyre gains normal force while the rear unloads. On a bicycle the front brake carries roughly 70–90 % of achievable deceleration, and the rear skids or lifts long before it runs out of brake torque.

So a rear regen brake becomes traction-limited early — you'd be building capture capacity you can't use. Front placement puts your energy recovery exactly where the braking energy actually flows.

Bonus — kit-ability

You asked for a drop-in kit that fits most bikes. Front is still dramatically easier: a front kit is "swap the wheel." Rear means matching dropout width (135 QR / 142×12 / 148 Boost), freehub standard (HG / XD / Micro Spline), speed count, derailleur setup — and now also drive-side caliper clearance.

Constraint that limits any kit — thru-axles

Grin's FAQ is blunt about this: their Freegen hubs use "a conventional 12 mm threaded axle with 10 mm flats" and cannot accommodate thru-axles via adapters. Any carrier-brake conversion built on an existing geared motor core inherits that limit. Since thru-axles dominate newer bikes, "fits most bikes" realistically means "fits most QR bikes" unless you go to a purpose-built axle. Worth deciding early — it shapes who the kit is for.

Non-negotiable safety item

Front hub + regen means axle torque reverses direction under braking. That is the classic dropout-ejection scenario. This kit needs torque arms on both sides, specified and shipped as part of it — not left to the installer. A steel fork is strongly preferred; aluminium and carbon forks need extra scrutiny. Grin sells purpose-built arms and has published extensively on the failure mode.

Worth saying plainly: if your goal were simply "regen braking on a bike," GMAC is the answer and you'd be done today. Your project is the carrier-brake concept — preserving the freewheel is the whole point, and that's a genuine advantage GMAC gives up. I'd build Path A and put a refundable deposit down as a hedge.

One more thing from Grin's FAQ that's relevant to you directly: "The control of both Freegen and Magic Drive requires very specialized motor controller firmware… You'd be free to try and develop your own code to implement these things if you are a controller engineer or an experienced firmware developer." That is precisely the work you've already been doing. Their controller will be closed; yours doesn't have to be.

Supercapacitor sizing — and why you need a friction fallback

You want supercaps rather than a battery, which is the right call for a brake buffer: brake energy arrives in short high-power bursts, which is exactly what caps are for. But the energy ceiling is low enough to shape the control design.

bank 20 F, usable 10 → 42 V energy E = ½CV² → ½·20·42² = 17 640 J at top ½·20·10² = 1 000 J at floor usable ≈ 16.6 kJ ≈ 4.6 Wh one stop 100 kg @ 25 km/h → ½·100·6.94² = 2 410 J ≈ 4 hard stops of storage at ~60 % capture descent 100 kg, 5 % grade, 30 km/h P = m·g·sinθ·v = 100·9.81·0.05·8.33 ≈ 409 W bank saturates in 16 600 / 409 ≈ 41 s
Design consequence — saturation

Forty seconds into any real descent your caps are full and regen tapers to nothing. The rider must not feel the brake disappear. Press coverage of Freegen describes the handover as stalling the motor to lock the carrier, converting the regen brake back into a friction brake with no lever-feel discontinuity. That's a secondary source, not Grin's own wording — but the mode is necessary regardless, and it argues for sizing the band to take full braking duty alone.

Different failure — dead electronics

Grin's FAQ answers this directly and the answer is not reassuring: "If your battery becomes detached or there is a critical failure of the electronics, then indeed the regen braking force on the associated hub will be lost. You would then come to a stop using the mechanical brake on your other wheel." With the clutch released, clamping the carrier just spins it — no reaction torque, no braking. They treat it as equivalent to snapping a cable on one brake.

A fail-safe worth designing in (my proposal, unverified): a normally-closed contactor across the three motor phases. On power loss it shorts the windings, so a clamped carrier back-drives the motor into a dead short and you get speed-proportional dynamic braking with no electronics alive at all. This needs real analysis — short-circuit torque at speed, contactor rating, and what happens if it closes at road speed with the carrier locked — but it converts Grin's "you lose that brake" into "you keep a degraded one," which matters a lot more on a front wheel.

Cell string: at 2.7 V/cell you need 16 cells for a 43 V ceiling, 18 for headroom to 48 V. For 20 F total from 16 in series, each cell must be ~320 F — so 16 × 350 F, 2.7 V is the natural build. Series strings must be balanced; the lowest-capacitance cell takes the highest voltage and degrades first, which cascades. Budget for per-cell balancing, not a resistor afterthought.

Missing hardware in v1

The old firmware has a PRECHARGE state that only inhibits motor commands — there is no GPIO anywhere driving a precharge relay. Connecting a discharged 20 F bank to anything is a dead short. v2 needs a real precharge circuit (series resistor plus a contactor that bypasses it once the bank is up) under MCU control, with the state machine actually driving it.

Carrier slip is derived, not measured directly: ω_carrier = (ω_motor + 4.8·ω_wheel) / 5.8. The wheel term carries 83 % of the weight in that expression against 17 % for the motor term, so wheel-speed resolution is the precision-critical spec in the whole system. A 3-magnet sensor is nowhere near adequate — you want a 32–64 pole magnetic ring counted in hardware by an RP2350 PIO block, which costs zero CPU.

Firmware — the shape of the rebuild

You're right that v1 is bloated. It runs about 3 200 lines across 21 modules, much of it scaffolding for a control strategy we're now replacing. Target for v2 is roughly 800 lines across 6 modules, and most of the reduction comes from deleting things rather than compressing them.

main.py

~80 lines
link.py
VESC transport. Zero-copy ring buffer, table CRC, live error counters.
~180 lines

Throttle, brake lever, wheel speed via PIO. Debounced validity on all three.
~140 lines

~150 lines

~120 lines

~130 lines

What disappears outright

  • The 1 kHz LispBM peak-hold script — and with it a timebase bug that made your tuned unlock_thresh meaningless
  • drpm_mean / drpm_peak_neg plumbing through four layers
  • AIMD probe-and-backoff and its four tuned parameters → a 2-gain slip PI
  • Polled telemetry request path → broadcast/push
  • REGEN_HOLDOFF_MS and the RPM entry/exit thresholds → lever sensor and slip
  • Strategy-selection indirection, the second LCD driver, vesc_config.py, the string-based enums
On testing

You flagged tests as bloat. Partly fair — but the 316 firmware tests run in 1.06 seconds and are genuinely cheap. What was bloated is that they were testing a control architecture we're deleting. v2 keeps a small suite over the things that are actually hard to get right: frame parsing and resync under injected garbage, the slip algebra, fault debouncing, and precharge sequencing. Perhaps 60–80 tests, not 340.

What I need from you before Phase 2

  1. Front or rear — final call. I recommend front, but now only on the braking-physics and simplicity arguments — the packaging objection turned out to be wrong. Rear carrier-braking is achievable with a drive-side caliper, at the cost of a custom mount, frame clearance, and a heavier motor. If you want rear and want it simple, GMAC remains the honest answer, and the carrier-brake concept goes away.
  2. QR-only, or do you need thru-axle? Geared motor cores come with 12 mm threaded axles and don't adapt to thru-axles. If the kit must fit modern thru-axle frames, that's a custom axle programme and a different class of project — worth knowing now rather than after the first wheel build.
  3. Can you open your Puyan H01 and photograph the carrier? Before buying anything I'd like to see the existing carrier, its clutch, and how much axial room there is for a brake surface. That tells us whether v2 reuses the motor or needs a new one.
  4. Hydraulic or mechanical brakes on the target bike? This decides the lever sensor — a hydraulic pressure sensor is by far the best signal, but only if the kit assumes hydraulic. Mechanical means a cable load cell or lever-travel hall, which is noisier.
  5. Budget ceiling for v2? The shortlist lands somewhere around $700–1600 CAD depending on how much you reuse. Knowing the ceiling changes whether I spec the Newhaven sunlight display or a generic IPS panel, and whether we go straight to CAN or start with RS-422.
